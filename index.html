Japan Through Bathing Culture

Exploring Japan’s bathing culture through the people, places and crafts that shape it.

From neighbourhood sentō and hot springs to ryokan, architecture, art and everyday rituals, the project explores how bathing culture developed, how it is lived today, and how it continues to change over time.

The project

A culture of bathing. A way of exploring Japan.

Bathing in Japan is more than a single practice or type of place. It connects public baths, hot springs and inns with everyday routines, etiquette, hospitality, communities, history and the people whose work makes these spaces possible.

This documentary project starts with the bath and follows those connections outward. I want to understand how these practices developed, what they mean to different people today, and how they are continued, adapted or reinterpreted over time.

The bath also opens onto a wider material world: murals, architecture, woodwork, ceramics, tableware, textiles, gardens, food and seasonal decoration. These details are not simply aesthetic. They can reveal the skills, choices and relationships that shape the places in which bathing culture exists.

The aim is not to arrive with a definition of what Japanese bathing culture “really is”. It is to listen to the people who live, work and create around it, and allow their perspectives to shape the episodes.

The world around the bath

One subject, many connected worlds.

The bath is the starting point, but rarely the whole story. Each place is shaped by the people who use and maintain it, the objects and skills around it, and the choices made over time. These are the connections the project follows.

01

Bath

The gestures, rules and sensations of bathing — and what they reveal about comfort, privacy and being together.

02

People

The people who bathe, welcome, clean, repair, manage and return — each with a different relationship to the same place.

03

Places

A neighbourhood sentō, mountain onsen or family ryokan can shape very different kinds of everyday life and encounter.

04

Making

The things around the bath are made by someone. Following them can uncover craft, labour and decisions usually hidden from the guest.

05

Time

What continues because it still works? What changes because people need it to? History becomes visible through those everyday choices.

Questions we are exploring

A working map, not a fixed programme.

The project is currently being researched as a series of independent episodes of roughly 20–25 minutes. These are working questions rather than a fixed programme: the final stories will depend on the people we meet, what we learn and the access available.

01

Why Japan Bathes

How did bathing acquire meanings beyond washing, and how can those histories still be understood today?

Rather than treating bathing as a timeless national habit, trace how ideas about cleanliness, healing, religion and shared water met — and changed.

02

The Public Bath

What place does a neighbourhood sentō hold in contemporary life?

If most homes have baths, why keep going to the sentō? The answer may lie as much in routine, ownership and neighbourhood life as in the water.

03

The Journey to the Hot Spring

How has travelling to hot springs changed — and how have onsen destinations changed with it?

From long therapeutic stays to short leisure trips, changing reasons for travelling to hot springs also change what towns must become for visitors — and residents.

04

The Inn

What makes ryokan hospitality possible every day?

Hospitality is visible to the guest; the labour behind it usually is not. Follow the people and decisions that make the experience possible each day.

05

The Hands Around the Bath

What can the things surrounding bathing tell us about the culture itself?

A mural, bathhouse tile or wooden fitting becomes more interesting when we can follow the maker, the commission and the place where it is actually used.

06

Continuity and Change

What do people choose to continue, adapt, reinterpret — or leave behind?

Continuity is not automatic — and change is not necessarily loss. Ask the people involved what deserves effort, what no longer fits, and who gets to decide.

Approach

Listening before explaining.

I am approaching this project as a curious observer rather than as an expert arriving with a conclusion. Research gives me questions, but the people I meet should be able to challenge those questions, contradict my assumptions and point the project somewhere unexpected.

Whenever possible, the episodes will favour observation over explanation: following people at work, watching how places function and connecting history to real objects, routines and spaces in the present.

Short contextual interludes

History, belief and folklore — when they illuminate the present.

Between observational sequences, short segments can connect a place, object or practice to a historical episode, local story, belief or material tradition. They are not decorative detours: each one should be sourced and anchored to something we can still see in the episode today.

People before themes

Start with specific experiences rather than using people to illustrate a predetermined idea.

Questions before conclusions

Treat preservation, change, tourism, community and tradition as things to investigate rather than positions to defend.

Context before spectacle

Avoid exoticising, romanticising or looking for shock effects.

Respect before access

Private spaces, bathing spaces and identifiable people are filmed only with appropriate permission and consent.

About the filmmaker

Research first.
Film with care.

I’m Arnaud, a French designer and researcher developing this documentary project independently.

For more than ten years, my professional work has involved behavioural and qualitative research applied to user experience — observing how people behave, listening to their experiences, asking questions and testing assumptions rather than beginning with an answer.

Documentary filmmaking is a newer practice for me, but that research mindset strongly shapes the way I approach this project: with curiosity, care and a willingness to let the people I meet challenge my initial understanding.

My interest in Japan has developed over many years, and this project comes from a desire to look beyond destinations themselves — toward the people, work, customs, objects and relationships that give places their meaning.

I am currently developing and funding the project independently, with the intention of first making the strongest episodes I can before deciding how the project should grow.
